The Five-Star Machine

A full census of 256,975 Google reviews across every Allianz agency listing in Germany and Switzerland. It shows a rating system that runs on solicitation and, in documented clusters, on agents rating each other — while the complaints that regulators receive keep climbing. 01 · Headline numbers

On Google, Germany's Allianz agents are nearly flawless

Across all 7,111 German Allianz agency listings, 96.1 percent of ratings are five stars. 3,660 listings — more than half — hold a perfect rounded 5.0. Negative ratings: 1.6 percent of a quarter million.

Switzerland's 107 listings look different: 9.3 percent negative, and one-star ratings alone (919) outnumber the two-, three- and four-star ratings combined (800). Same company, same decade — a six-fold difference in visible criticism. The sections below take that gap apart.

02 · The 2025 surge

Review volume tripled, then tripled again

German Allianz listings collected 24,044 reviews in 2024, 68,416 in 2025, and 73,891 in just the first 220 days of 2026 — an annualized pace of roughly 122,000, five times the 2024 level. November 2025 was the first month above 10,000; most months since have stayed there. Switzerland bent the same way at smaller scale: 902 reviews in 2024, 2,274 in 2025.

Word-of-mouth does not behave like this. A doubling-then-doubling curve across thousands of independent listings is what organized solicitation looks like: rate-us links in follow-up mails, QR codes on counters, campaigns pushed through the agent network. Asking for reviews is legal. But from early 2025 the star average of a German Allianz agency stopped being a passive measurement and became a managed number — which matters for anyone using it to pick an insurer.

03 · What the stars rate

Five stars grade the buying. One star grades the paying.

Reading the texts, not just counting them: in Germany 55 percent of five-star texts praise the advisor or the sales consultation; only 9 percent mention a claim, payout or the word Schaden. Among negative texts, claims and payouts are the dominant substantive topic in both countries — 13 percent in Germany, 26 percent in Switzerland — alongside silence: unanswered mails, unreturned calls.

So the two ends of the scale describe different products. The five-star mass rates the pleasant afternoon a policy was sold; the one-star tail rates what happened when money had to flow the other way. An average blending the two mostly measures the first.

04 · The negative reviews

What the critics say, in their own words

Multilingual keyword analysis over every negative text — 2,721 in Germany, 675 in Switzerland after removing one mass-posting profile (section 5). Germany's complaints lead with silence (11 percent), then cancellation disputes (8 percent). Switzerland's lead with cancellation by the insurer (11 percent — frequently after claims were filed, a practice Swiss consumer TV has documented at Allianz Suisse[38]), then silence, then denied or reduced claims (6 percent, about twice the German rate).

Discrimination: what the data can and cannot say

Explicit allegations of racism or discrimination appear in 9 of 2,721 German negative texts and 3 of 716 Swiss ones — individual, unadjudicated accounts, two quoted above. No public statistic measures discrimination in claims handling: neither ombudsman, nor BaFin, nor FINMA publishes complaint breakdowns by discrimination ground or by insurer[26][28]. Adjacent evidence exists: Comparis found drivers with Kosovar, North Macedonian or Turkish passports paying up to 74 percent more for Swiss motor cover[35], and Germany's anti-discrimination agency documented age-based pricing disadvantages in 2026 testing[36]. For claims settlement itself, the public record is simply empty — a gap worth naming rather than papering over.

Language of the critics

Review language is not nationality, but it is measurable. In Germany, negative reviews are written in German at nearly the same rate as positive ones, and Arabic-script reviews skew strongly positive (359 positive, 17 negative — clustered at agencies with Arabic-speaking advisors). In Switzerland, English-language reviews are about twice as frequent among negatives (5.7%) as among positives (2.6%); French is mildly overrepresented too. Expat customers appear less happy — or less inhibited about saying so.

05 · Two kinds of flooding

A flood of praise and a flood of anger are not the same thing

Both directions of review distortion exist in this dataset. They are not symmetrical, and treating them as equivalent would be a mistake.

When one customer floods negatives, the act is loud, traceable and self-limiting: it comes from an identifiable profile, it violates Google's locality rule the moment it spreads beyond the place of the actual experience, and a single flagging round removes it. There is no revenue in it. It is, almost by definition, the behaviour of someone who believes ordinary channels have failed — a symptom that a case went badly wrong, whatever the merits underneath.

When praise floods, the economics reverse. The beneficiary is a business; the authors are many, dispersed and unremarkable one by one; nobody flags flattery, so it survives moderation indefinitely and compounds into the rating that customers, comparison sites and Google's own ranking treat as truth. One is a scream; the other is infrastructure.

The dataset makes the asymmetry measurable. Using the definitions in section 10, reviews bearing suspect-positive patterns (landing in a statistical burst week, or written by profiles that rated five or more different Allianz agencies) total 35,114 across both countries — 14.3 percent of all five-star reviews. Reviews from campaign-negative profiles (negatives spread across three or more listings) total 67, and 41 of those are one Swiss profile. Five hundred twenty-four to one.

The one negative flood in this data — read as a case file

Switzerland, 2026. A customer describes a theft claim ("I've claimed a Diebstahl from my garage already back in November 2025 and there's no update, no transparency since then… If I'm not writing daily mails or at least one per week I'm not getting any help") and posts a one-star review on the head-office listing in January. Two months of silence later, the same profile pastes a near-identical one-star text — now alleging the insurer is "spreading completely false allegations" against the customer — onto 39 agency listings, almost all within eight March days, reaching 41 Swiss listings by May. That one profile is 39 of the 56 Swiss negative reviews in March 2026; the monthly chart's apparent reputation collapse is mostly this.

The mass-posting violates Google's locality rule and is removable[14]. But as evidence, its interesting content is the trajectory: claim filed, months of alleged non-response, escalation into the only channel left that anyone reads. That trajectory matches the dominant complaint themes of the Swiss negatives (silence, denial, being treated as the fraudster — section 4), matches what BaFin says drove its 2025 complaint record in Germany (slow claims handling[29]), and matches the escalation ladder consumer bodies describe. A company that resolves claims does not usually acquire carpet-bombing critics. The flood is the fever, not the disease.

06 · Exhibits

Five patterns, documented

Each exhibit states the pattern, the rule it strains against, and the most plausible origin — including whether the fingerprints point at Allianz's own network or at enthusiastic customers. Verification links lead to the live Google listings. Individuals are identified by initials here; the raw dataset contains the public names. None of this is a court finding; all of it is checkable.

Exhibit A

Agency owners rating each other — 88 reciprocal pairs, 30 on the same day

We matched reviewer names against the owner names embedded in Allianz's own listing titles ("Allianz Generalvertretung [Name]"). 376 owner-named reviewer profiles rated two or more other Allianz agencies — 1,459 agency ratings, averaging 4.93. The sharpest slice: 88 pairs of agency owners who rated each other's agencies, and in 30 of those pairs the two five-star reviews were posted on the same day. Eleven more within 48 hours. Same-day reciprocity is what a "you rate me, I rate you" exchange looks like; coincidence does not schedule itself like that. The pattern also survives the common-name objection: restricted to distinctive surnames only, 62 pairs remain, 27 of them same-day-or-adjacent.

Tied agents of the same insurer exchanging five-star ratings on each other's public listings, often same-day; one owner's name appears as reviewer on 28 other agencies.

Exhibit B

Touring five-star profiles — 73 agencies, 38 cities, one reviewer

49 reviewer profiles rated ten or more different Allianz agencies, essentially always five stars. The most active rated 73 agencies across 38 cities. One profile rated 33 agencies within 30 days in late 2025 — a new agency every 22 hours, spanning most of Germany. These profiles match no owner name; they are also not plausible customers. No private household maintains relationships with 73 Allianz agencies. The candidate explanations left standing: field staff, sales-structure colleagues, trainers, or paid accounts. From outside, the data cannot distinguish between those — it can only rule out the innocent one.

Exhibit C

The harvest weekend — 150 reviews in 48 hours at one agency

A Bavarian agency's listing[41] collected 91 reviews on Sunday 13 April 2025 and 59 more on the Monday — 166 that week, 133 of them without text. The texted ones locate the operation: reviewers thank the agency for the nice chat at the BMW Open, the Munich tennis tournament running that weekend. A booth, a QR code, and a listing whose 4.87 average now rests mostly on one weekend's foot traffic — 214 reviews total, over three-quarters of them from those days. This is the mildest exhibit: real people probably pressed the buttons. It still shows how a "customer rating" can be manufactured wholesale at an event, and if stars were rewarded or selectively encouraged, it crosses from marketing into violation[6][14].

Exhibit D

Stars after scars — five-star inflow jumps 61% right after a negative

For each of the 3,952 dated negative reviews in Germany we counted the listing's five-star reviews in the fourteen days before and after. Before: 0.57 on average. After: 0.91 — a 61 percent jump, measured symmetrically around each event, so background growth cannot produce it. In 193 episodes the response was flagrant: six or more five-stars within a fortnight at four-plus times the listing's pace. Extreme case: one negative on a Biberach listing[42] in August 2025 was followed by 55 five-star reviews in fourteen days, where the listing's baseline predicted about two. The pattern reads as reactive solicitation — bad review appears, the "ask happy customers" machinery starts. Google's policy name for the deliberate version, steering positives in while criticism stays out, is review gating, and it is prohibited[14].

Exhibit E

The head-office wave — 2,546 reviews in four years, mostly silent five-stars

Allianz Suisse's own head-office listing in Wallisellen[43] holds 4,815 reviews — 44 percent of the entire Swiss dataset — at 87 percent five-star. More than half arrived in a 2018–2021 wave; 62 percent of the listing's five-star ratings have no text. An insurance head office is where people fight about claims, not where they drop happy ratings — the same company's Trustpilot page sits deep in the red[44]. 867 five-star ratings in the single year 2020 is the shape of a run campaign (NPS mail, app prompt, or similar), not of spontaneous gratitude. After the wave ends in 2022, volume falls to roughly a third of its peak and the negative share more than doubles.

07 · The law

Which of this is illegal, which is merely ugly

The legal line runs between asking and faking, paying, or filtering. Since Germany implemented the EU Omnibus Directive in May 2022, fake reviews are per-se unfair; Switzerland reaches the same result via its unfair-competition act — with criminal liability on complaint.

The rules

  • Legal Asking customers for reviews — mails, QR codes, event booths — if everyone is asked and nothing is promised in return.
  • Prohibited Fake or commissioned reviews: UWG Anhang Nr. 23c; presenting reviews as verified-genuine without verification: Nr. 23b[1]. Platforms and businesses displaying reviews must disclose whether and how authenticity is checked: §5b(3)[2].
  • Prohibited Undisclosed incentivized reviews — vouchers (OLG Frankfurt 2022)[6], prize draws (OLG Frankfurt 2024)[7]; hidden paid reviews (OLG Frankfurt 2019)[4]; the fake-review broker Fivestar Marketing was ordered shut out by LG München I[5].
  • Switzerland Misleading reviews: UWG Art. 2 and Art. 3(1)(b); intentional violations criminally punishable on complaint, Art. 23[10]. A 2021 SRF investigation found Swiss sellers offering 50 five-star reviews for CHF 400[11].
  • Platform Google prohibits fake engagement, any incentive for reviews, conflict-of-interest reviews and review gating; it reports 170M+ policy-violating reviews removed in 2023[14][16].
  • Precedent Insurance has form: wefox's call centers solicited positive Google reviews against Amazon vouchers of up to €30, lifting its rating from 2.5 to 4.5 stars[17][18]. No documented case names Allianz or its agencies; our search found none — absence of a case, not proof of absence.

Enforcement is the weak joint. Germany polices this mainly through civil claims by competitors, the Wettbewerbszentrale and consumer associations; general fines exist only in coordinated cross-border cases (§19 UWG, up to €50,000 or 4 percent of turnover)[3]. Nobody audits star averages of their own motion. A listing can quintuple its review volume in two years without a single official question — as 7,111 of them just demonstrated.

08 · Where to report

No register exists. These channels do.

There is no public database of review-manipulation complaints in Germany or Switzerland — nothing to search for prior reports against a company. The nearest public artifact is the EU's DSA Transparency Database of platform moderation decisions[9].

Channels

  • Platform Google: flag individual reviews via the three-dot menu, or the business reviews-management tool[15]. Off-topic mass posts and conflict-of-interest reviews are removable categories.
  • Germany Wettbewerbszentrale complaint office (can warn and sue; not anonymous); Verbraucherzentralen; Bundesnetzagentur only as Digital Services Coordinator if a platform ignores notices (dsc.bund.de).
  • Switzerland Lauterkeitskommission — free complaints, published decisions[12]; SECO — can sue or file criminal complaints where collective interests are affected[13].
09 · The bigger picture

While the stars rose, the complaints rose too

The years of the review surge are also record years at the complaint desks. The German insurance ombudsman logged 21,548 complaints in 2024 and 28,904 in 2025 — records both years[26][27]. About 73 percent of 2024's complaints were admissible; of those decided, 34 percent (life) to 52 percent (other lines) ended at least partly in the customer's favour[26]. BaFin's insurance complaints jumped in 2025, driven by slow claims handling[29] — the same theme that tops the negative reviews. Switzerland's ombudsman handled 3,336 matters in 2024; of 1,254 written complaints, 299 led to intervention[28]. Neither ombudsman publishes per-insurer numbers; BaFin's per-company table shows Allianz mid-field (life: 13 complaints per 100k contracts)[25]. No official claims-denial rate exists in either country; for German occupational-disability cover, approval of decided claims runs near 80 percent by industry count, lower once abandoned claims are included[30].

When the insurer calls the customer the liar

Several Swiss negatives — and the trigger text of the flood in section 5 — describe being treated as a fraud suspect. That machinery is real and industrial: Germany's insurers run the HIS warning file (reportedly around 10 million entries; courts have ordered wrongful entries deleted)[31]; Switzerland launched its HIS in 2021 for the largest non-life insurers, ten entry reasons, seven-year retention[32]. Art. 40 VVG lets a Swiss insurer void the contract for a fraudulent claim — and the Federal Supreme Court recently had to restrain how easily "fraud" may be proven (4A_394/2021)[33]. The industry's claim that one in ten claims is suspicious is its own estimate, not an adjudicated fact[34].

  • Germany Market leader — 17.5% of gross premiums, #1 in life and P&C[21]; roughly 8,000 tied agencies[22] — the listings this dataset is made of.

10 · Method

Definitions, honestly, and what this cannot prove

Data. Full census of Google reviews on Allianz agency and office listings: 7,111 German listings / 246,116 reviews (collected 8 Aug 2026), 107 Swiss listings / 10,859 reviews (21 Jul – 7 Aug 2026). Scraped counts match Google's displayed star totals; timestamps are exact dates.

Definitions. Burst week: ≥8 five-star reviews within 7 days at ≥4× the listing's own average weekly pace. Suspect-positive: a five-star review inside a burst week, or by a profile that rated ≥5 different Allianz agencies at ≥4.5 average. Campaign-negative: a 1–2★ review by a profile posting negatives on ≥3 listings. Owner match: reviewer name equals the owner name in another listing's title; common-surname caveat applies and is quantified in Exhibit A. Themes: multilingual regex buckets (DE/FR/IT/EN) over negative texts. Language identification is automatic and noisy below ~40 characters.

Limits. Google shows only what moderation left standing; removed reviews are invisible, which biases the record toward surviving manipulation. Reviewer identity cannot be verified from outside — a name match is strong circumstantial evidence at same-day reciprocity scale, and weak alone. Burst and after-negative patterns are screening indicators of solicitation, not proof of purchase. Quoted allegations are the reviewers' own. Individuals appear here as initials; the dataset preserves public names and profile URLs so every exhibit can be independently reproduced. This report names patterns and tests explanations; it convicts nobody.
